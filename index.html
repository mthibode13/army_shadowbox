Army Hat Crest - In Detail

What: This is the officer's garrison cap insignia for the U.S. Army. It features the Great Seal of the United States. It's worn on the left side of the garrison cap.

When: Worn throughout an officer's career with the garrison cap. This particular one represents general officer service.

Where: Used wherever the garrison cap is the prescribed headgear.

Who: Worn by U.S. Army officers.

Name Plate - In Detail

What: Standard U.S. Army nameplate, typically black with white lettering, displaying the wearer's last name. It's worn on the Army Service Uniform (ASU), previously on the Army Green Class A uniform and other service uniforms.

When: Worn throughout an individual's Army career when in the appropriate uniform.

Where: Positioned on the right side of the uniform coat for males, and aligned with the bottom edge of the nameplate to the top of the top button for females on the ASU coat. Specific regulations dictate placement.

Army Service Ribbon (ASR) - In Detail

What: The Army Service Ribbon (ASR) is awarded to members of the U.S. Army for successful completion of initial-entry training. It signifies that a soldier has completed the necessary basic training to become a functional member of the Army.

When: Typically awarded after graduating from Advanced Individual Training (AIT). For officers, after completion of their basic officer leader course.

Where: Awarded at the training installation where initial-entry training was completed.

Who: All members of the Army (Active Duty, Reserves, National Guard) are eligible upon meeting the criteria.

2nd Lieutenant - Commissioning & Early Career (1989-1990)

Georgia Military College & Commissioning

Competed for an ROTC scholarship at Officer's Basic Camp, Fort Knox. Offered scholarship at Georgia Military College (GMC), Milledgeville. Commissioned in 1989.

Officer's Basic Course & First Steps

What: Attended Officer's Basic Course at Fort Gordon, Augusta, GA for Signal Corps. Specialized in computers and electronics.

When: 1990 (after a 6-month break post-commissioning).

Where: Fort Gordon, Augusta, GA.

Key Locations: Early Career

Milledgeville (GMC) and Augusta (Fort Gordon) were pivotal starting points.

Significance & Early Path

Graduated in the top 20% of the OBC class. This period set the stage for an initial assignment as a Battalion Signal Officer, a deviation from a desired infantry path but an area of aptitude.

Full Narrative: The Beginning

After graduating high school in 1987, I competed for a two-year ROTC scholarship at the Officer's Basic Camp at Fort Knox and was fortunate to earn one to Georgia Military (Junior) College (GMC). My path to GMC was somewhat serendipitous: I had sent my ACT scores there on a whim, simply because there were free slots to use when I took the test. While scanning college lists—excluding local options like USL (now UL) and LSU—I specifically sought out military colleges, as my hopes for the Naval Academy had been dashed by a height requirement I read about in a brochure.

That summer, my attempt to join the Marine Corps was thwarted by an ASVAB score deemed too low. Subsequently, I received mail offers for the enlisted submarine track, which didn't align with my aspirations. The turning point came with a call from Major Toler at GMC. He offered me an academic scholarship based on my ACT scores and GPA, guaranteeing it and adding that strong performance at the mandatory summer camp could lead to an ROTC scholarship. I accepted the challenge, and alongside about nine peers, I made it through. Life in Milledgeville was tough, but feeling I couldn't go back home, I was determined to make the most of every opportunity.

As GMC had an early commissioning program, I was commissioned after just two years. Upon graduating and swearing in, I drove back to Louisiana, hoping to join the Louisiana Infantry with my new commission. However, they had no vacant Infantry platoon positions. Instead, they offered me a role as a battalion signal officer. I embraced the opportunity and, in retrospect, was glad I did. My land navigation skills were only just passing, and I likely would have been a mediocre Infantry officer. In contrast, my keen interest in computers and electronics made the Signal Corps assignment a genuinely enjoyable fit.

In 1990, while finishing my bachelor's degree at LSU and having just joined my unit, I learned I needed to qualify at the Officer's Basic Course at Fort Gordon, Augusta, Georgia, or risk losing my unit position and free tuition. I took a six-month break from school to attend the course, did well, and graduated in the top 20% of my class.

Immediately following the basic course, I went onto active duty with the 2nd Battalion, 256th Infantry Brigade (Mechanized), a roundout brigade for the 5th Infantry Division based out of Fort Polk. We mobilized and conducted training from squad to battalion levels, but by the time we were ready, the Gulf War had concluded. Interestingly, according to General Norman Schwarzkopf's aide-de-camp, General BB Bell, the U.S. objective had been to conclude the conflict faster than Israel's victory in the Six Day War. The U.S. campaign lasted 100 hours before Saddam Hussein's surrender was accepted.
